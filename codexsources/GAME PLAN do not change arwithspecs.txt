### Phase 2: Ideation — The Art of the Surgical Strike

This is where you win or lose. A generic "AR Wikipedia" will be ignored. The past winning examples—Lengua Learn (language translation), YEIGO (mobility aid), Arcad (measurement tool)—all share a common DNA: **They are tools for a specific task, used by a specific person, in a specific context**.

**Your Winning Idea Framework: The "Occupied Hands" Principle.**

The single most powerful filter for a SPECS idea is: **The user’s hands are busy, and their eyes need to stay on a real-world task.** This is the core value proposition of glasses.

**Do not build:**
- ❌ A floating social media feed (solves nothing a phone can’t do better).
- ❌ A virtual pet (novelty, not a tool).
- ❌ A generic "information dashboard" (vague user, vague context).

**Instead, find a niche like this:**
- **"SolderCoach"**: While soldering a PCB, both hands are holding an iron and solder wire. The user cannot look at a laptop schematic. An AR overlay highlights the correct pin, shows the thermal dwell timer, and provides a step card *in situ*.
- **"BioSurg AR"**: A hands-free surgical copilot that provides vascular mapping or trauma protocols without the surgeon breaking sterile field or looking away from the patient.
- **"Shake Lab"**: An interactive structural-engineering workbench to visualize resonance and dampers on a physical model, hands-free.

**Your Task:** Have your apprentice run the `plan-lens` skill. Let it interview them. But before they answer, they must have a candidate idea ready that fits the "Occupied Hands" principle. The skill pack will validate it against AR best practices, but it won’t invent a great idea for you.

**The Design Brief (The Most Important Document):**
The `devpost/design-brief.md` file that the skill pack generates is your blueprint. It must contain:
- **The Spatial Moment:** A one-sentence description of the user, their task, and why AR is the only solution (e.g., "A technician repairing a server rack needs both hands to swap a hot-swap drive while reading the RAID configuration status without looking away.").
- **The "Found" Stand-In:** Since you don’t have SPECS, your preview must simulate the real world. Define a "Found" button or trigger in your scene that represents the real object the user is looking at. This proves you understand the context.
- **Placement Numbers:** Explicitly state distances in cm from the user’s eye. "The primary status indicator is anchored 65cm away, 15cm below eye level." This shows technical rigor.
- **Done Criteria:** 3-4 binary, testable criteria. Example: "Done Criterion 1: When the 'Start' button is pinched, a 3.5-second countdown ring appears over Pin 1." This becomes your LEAF test script.

### Phase 3: Development — CLAD as Your Co-Developer

Do not ask CLAD for "an AR app." You are the architect; it is the builder.

**The Workflow:**
1.  **Scaffold the Scene:** Use `/scene-construction` to place your primary anchor point in the preview. Verify its position against your design brief numbers.
2.  **Build Assets:** Use `/build-mesh` for any custom 3D objects (a floating pin highlight, a step card frame). Use `/shader-graph` for visual affordances (a subtle pulse on an active target).
3.  **Construct the UI:** Use `/specs-build-ui`. The Spectacles Interaction Kit (SIK) and UI Kit provide pre-built prefabs like `PinchButton`, `ToggleButton`, and `Slider`. **Insist that CLAD uses these prefabs.** Custom UI that mimics the system language is a red flag for "Technical Implementation." Prefer prefabs first.
4.  **Wire the Logic:** Use `/lens-api` and the `script-author` agent to write the TypeScript controller. The logic should be simple and robust. Follow the event model of SIK (events bubble down the scene graph).
5.  **Test, Iterate, Refine:** Use the `/verify-preview` skill after *every* change. Watch the Lens Studio preview panel like a hawk. CLAD can simulate camera movement, so make it test your lens from slightly off-center angles to ensure your content is robust.

**The "Gotcha" to Avoid:** CLAD might try to "one-shot" your entire lens. Stop it. Use the individual skills. A one-shotted lens is often generic and lacks the polish of a designed experience. You want a "complete, coherent product experience," not a "technical proof of concept".

### Phase 4: The Proof — LEAF Testing and Repo Hygiene

The judges will not run your project. The video is your proof. But the *repo* is where you prove the video isn’t a fluke.

**1. LEAF is Your Quality Seal:**
- After your lens works in preview, invoke the `live-lens-tester` agent. It will write a re-runnable LEAF (Lens Evaluation & Automation Framework) test script based on your Done Criteria.
- Run the tests in the LEAF panel (`Window > LEAF`). **You want a "PASS" on every single test.** Mention this in your submission. "4/4 LEAF Done Criteria passed" is a powerful statement of technical maturity.

**2. Repository Structure is Non-Negotiable:**
Your public repo must be clean and self-explanatory. Judges will glance at it.
- `/devpost/`: Contains `design-brief.md` (the approved plan).
- `/Assets/Scripts/`: Contains your controller script.
- `/Tests/`: Contains your LEAF test script.
- `.mcp.json`: The Lens Studio MCP configuration file.
- `YourProject.esproj`: The Lens Studio project file.
- `README.md`: A concise explanation of the project, linking to the video and the design brief.

### Phase 5: The Kill Shot — The 3-Minute Demo Video

This is your entire competition. The judges will watch this video and may not look at anything else before scoring. It must be perfect.

**Video Structure (180 Seconds Hard Limit):**

- **0:00 - 0:20 | The Hook (The "Occupied Hands" Problem).**
    - **Visual:** A tight shot of hands performing a delicate real-world task (e.g., soldering, cooking, repairing). The hands are busy. The person is looking down.
    - **Audio:** "When you're soldering a $2,000 board, you can't look at a laptop. But you need the schematic. That's the problem."
    - **On-screen text:** The project name. Keep it clean.

- **0:20 - 0:40 | The Solution Introduction.**
    - **Visual:** Transition to the Lens Studio preview screen recording.
    - **Audio:** "This is [Project Name]. It puts the information directly on the pin, right where you're working. No hands. No looking away."

- **0:40 - 2:20 | The End-to-End Demo (The "Money Shot").**
    - **Visual:** Smooth, unedited screen recording of the Lens Studio preview.
    - **Sequence:**
        1.  Show the "Found" button being pressed (simulating the user looking at the object).
        2.  The primary anchor point appears *precisely* on the simulated object.
        3.  Pinch to interact with a button. The UI responds immediately and clearly.
        4.  Show the core feature working (e.g., a timer starts, a status panel expands).
        5.  Pan the camera view slightly to the left and right to prove spatial stability. The content stays anchored.
    - **Audio:** Narrate the user journey. "The user pinches 'Start'. A countdown appears over the active pin. They complete the task. The timer verifies the dwell time. The interface never leaves their field of view."
    - **On-screen text:** Call out the "Design Decision" behind each step. "Anchored at 65cm for comfort." "SIK PinchButton for zero-learning-curve interaction."

- **2:20 - 2:50 | The Technical Credibility.**
    - **Visual:** Quick cuts showing the `design-brief.md` in your repo and the LEAF test panel passing.
    - **Audio:** "Built with CLAD. Planned with the skill pack. Verified with LEAF. The design brief, planning docs, and tests are all in the public repo."

- **2:50 - 3:00 | The Close.**
    - **Visual:** Your project name and a clean shot of the preview.
    - **Audio:** "For technicians who need their hands free and their eyes up. That's [Project Name]."
    - **On-screen text:** Link to the repo and a thank you.
